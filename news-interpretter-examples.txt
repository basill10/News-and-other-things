Story 1: Net Metering vs Net Billing
NET METERING VS NET BILLING — WHAT JUST HAPPENED?
The News
This week, Pakistan’s power regulator, the National Electric Power Regulatory Authority (NEPRA), approved a major change for rooftop solar users.
Pakistan is moving from net metering to net billing.
Here’s what that means in simple terms.
Under the old system — net metering — if your solar panels produced 10 units during the day, and you used 10 units at night, your bill roughly balanced out. You were effectively exchanging units with the grid on a one-to-one basis.
Now, under net billing, that exchange is no longer equal.
If you send electricity to the grid, it’s bought at a lower fixed rate — reported around Rs 11 per unit for new users. But when you take electricity from the grid, you still pay the full slab rate — often Rs 25 to Rs 40 or more.
So the electricity you sell is cheaper than the electricity you buy.
That’s the structural shift.
The announcement has triggered strong criticism. 


Critics argue this shift didn’t happen in a vacuum. Many say the government is now trying to fix past policy excesses and unfavourable contracts that made net metering too generous for a stressed grid. But others warn that rather than addressing core problems — like IPP costs, cross subsidies, and distribution inefficiencies — the reform shifts burden onto ordinary consumers and undermines confidence in renewable investment.


It is being said that, Instead of solving the structural problems within the energy system, the policy change might push consumers off-grid, accelerate battery adoption, and further weaken the grid
Why This Matters
So why does this matter?
Because this isn’t just about solar bills — it’s about the structure of Pakistan’s power system.
In the short term, this likely improves cash flows for distribution companies. The gap between what they pay solar users and what they charge consumers narrows.
But in the medium to long term, behavior changes.
If exporting excess electricity becomes less attractive, households may choose to store it instead — accelerating lithium battery adoption and reducing reliance on the grid.
That shifts Pakistan further toward a hybrid system: part centralized grid, part decentralized solar plus storage.
So this decision doesn’t end rooftop solar.
But it changes the incentives — and that may reshape how power is produced, stored, and consumed in urban Pakistan over the next few years.










Story 2: The Economics of Basant
THE ECONOMICS OF BASANT
The News
After nearly 25 years, Basant returned to Lahore last weekend as a government-regulated, pilot event.
It was banned in 2007 following fatalities linked to metal and glass-coated strings
Now it’s back — but under the Punjab Regulation of Kite Flying Act 2025:
* Only 9-thread cotton string allowed
* Metal, nylon, chemical and glass strings were banned
* QR-coded kites
* Zonal monitoring (red/yellow/green zones)
* Fines up to Rs 5 million and jail terms for violations
And the demand?
* Rs 540 million worth of kites sold within days
* Rooftops rented out for up to Rs 2 million
* Karachi–Lahore air tickets crossed Rs 75,000 one way
* Around 540 manufacturing units registered for production
This is not just nostalgia, for a fondly remembered spring festival.
This is serious economic activity.
Why It Matters
This is what economists call cultural economics.
Estimates vary — but even conservative adjustments indicate that Basant generated between Rs 5–6 billion in total activity, with the sale of kites and strings alone generating around Rs. 2 billion.
Some projections this year go as high as Rs 20 billion in overall economic impact
Where does that money flow?
* Kite manufacturing clusters (Shahdara, Baghbanpura)
* Paper & bamboo supply chains (historically up to 7% of paper consumption in the 1960s)
* Hospitality & hotel occupancy
* Food vendors
* Transport
* Rooftop rentals
* Media & corporate sponsorships
Historically, estimates suggest 10–15 lakh livelihoods were linked to the festival economy
This is a 3-day event activating multiple micro-industries simultaneously.
And there’s a second layer:
Soft power.
In the late 1990s, Basant was used to counter Pakistan’s global hardline image — becoming part of Lahore’s international branding
The economic question isn’t:
“Is Basant emotional?”
The real question is:
Can Pakistan afford to permanently shut down multi-billion-rupee cultural industries —
or should it regulate them better?








Story 3: Pakistan's $1b AI bet
PAKISTAN’S $1 BILLION AI BET
The News
At Indus AI Week 2026, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced that Pakistan will invest $1 billion in the AI sector by 2030.
The pledge is positioned around:
* AI research and PhD scholarships
* National AI curriculum integration
* Compute and infrastructure investment
* Startup ecosystem support
* Public sector AI adoption
Coupled with the National AI Policy that came out last year, we can see that the government is framing AI not just as a tech initiative — but as a national productivity lever.
Spread over four years, that’s roughly $250 million per year.
The headline is big.
But the interpretation matters more than the number.
Why This Matters
This isn’t just a tech story.
It’s an industrial policy story.
Countries that win in AI aren’t just building apps.
They’re building:
* Compute infrastructure
* Skilled workforces
* Data governance systems
* Domestic AI firms
Pakistan is signaling it wants to move from:
AI consumption → AI production.
But the real question is:
Is this $1B catalytic capital — or symbolic capital?
Because AI’s economic impact isn’t chatbots.
It’s:
* Manufacturing efficiency
* Supply chain optimization
* Agriculture yield modeling
* Government service digitization
* Better tax administration
If executed well, AI investment could:
* Improve export competitiveness
* Reduce leakage in public finance
* Increase firm-level productivity
But AI without power reliability, cloud infrastructure, and data access has limits.
And execution is everything.
Who controls the fund?
Is this public money or blended finance?
Grant-based or venture-based?
Are we building sovereign compute — or renting foreign infrastructure?
Historically, Pakistan has announced ambitious tech roadmaps before.
So clarity will determine whether $250M a year transforms capability — or disperses into projects.
And here’s the bigger tension:
Pakistan is simultaneously:
* Struggling with energy pricing
* Managing IMF conditionality
* Balancing fiscal consolidation
* Trying to grow exports
And now — betting on AI.
So the core question becomes:
Can a country still building its industrial base leapfrog into AI?
Or is AI the tool that upgrades that base?
